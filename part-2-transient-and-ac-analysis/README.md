# Part II — Transient and AC Analysis

Part I treated circuits whose voltages and currents are fixed, instant by instant, by the sources acting at that instant. Capacitors and inductors break that rule: they remember. A circuit containing them responds to a switching event with a transient that may take microseconds or minutes to die away, and responds to a sinusoidal source with currents that lead or lag the voltage. This part develops the two complementary ways of handling such circuits. The first is the time domain: write the differential equation, find its natural and forced responses, and match initial conditions — the method of switching transients, relay drive, power-on reset, ringing and damping. The second is the sinusoidal steady state: replace the differential equation by complex algebra with phasors and impedances — the method of mains power, audio and radio-frequency circuits, filters and three-phase systems. Between them they cover nearly every analysis an engineer performs on a linear circuit before reaching for transforms.

By the end of this part you should understand:

- why one storage element gives a single exponential with one time constant, and how the initial value, final value and time constant determine any first-order step response;
- how two storage elements exchange energy, and how the damping ratio and natural frequency decide between overdamped, critically damped and underdamped (ringing) responses;
- how phasors and complex impedance turn the sinusoidal steady state into the resistive circuit methods of Part I, including nodal and mesh analysis and the network theorems;
- what RMS values, active, reactive, apparent and complex power mean, why power factor matters on an electricity bill, and how it is corrected;
- how balanced and unbalanced three-phase systems are analysed, why the neutral carries current, and how three-phase power is measured.

The order is deliberate. Chapters 6 and 7 work entirely in the time domain and establish the vocabulary — natural frequency, time constant, damping, steady state versus transient — that the rest of the book reuses under other names: the natural frequencies become poles in Chapter 11, the step and impulse responses become the system descriptions of Chapters 15–17, and the damping ratio becomes a control specification in Chapters 34 and 35. Chapters 8–10 then specialise to sinusoidal excitation, the case on which the electricity supply, most measurement and all of radio rest. The phasor methods of Chapter 8 are the first appearance of frequency-domain thinking; Chapter 13 extends them into Bode plots and filters, Chapter 14 into transmission lines, and Chapter 18 into the Fourier transform. The power concepts of Chapters 9 and 10 are the circuit-theory foundation that *Electrical Energy & Machines* builds on for generators, transformers and power systems. Part II thus stands between the static circuit laws of Part I and the general network and signal theory of Parts III and IV: it is where circuits first acquire time and frequency.
