# Part I — Circuit Fundamentals

Every later part of this book rests on a small set of ideas about how electrical quantities behave when components are joined by wires. Part I builds that foundation. It defines the quantities an engineer measures (charge, current, voltage, power, energy), fixes the sign conventions that keep calculations honest, and states the lumped-element assumptions under which a physical gadget may be replaced by a drawing of ideal elements. On that basis it develops Kirchhoff's laws, the systematic nodal and mesh methods that solve any linear resistive network, the theorems that compress a network into a simple equivalent at its terminals, and finally the two energy-storing elements, the capacitor and the inductor, which bring time into the picture. The examples range from medical wearables and car electrical systems to building wiring, strain-gauge bridges and amplifier models, because the same few laws govern all of them.

By the end of this part you should understand:

- what current, voltage, power and energy measure, and how reference directions and the passive sign convention decide whether an element supplies or absorbs power;
- when the lumped-element model is valid, and which physics is missing when it fails;
- how ideal, practical and dependent sources model batteries, supplies, sensors, transistors and op-amps;
- why Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws hold, and how they combine with Ohm's law to give series and parallel rules, voltage and current division, source transformations and power balance;
- how nodal, mesh and modified nodal analysis turn any linear network into a matrix equation, and when that equation has exactly one solution;
- how superposition, Thévenin, Norton and the other network theorems summarise a network at its terminals, and where they stop applying;
- how capacitors and inductors store energy, why their voltages and currents cannot jump, and how coupled coils interact.

## How this part connects to the rest of the book

Part I treats circuits mostly at a single instant or in DC steady state. Part II (Transient and AC Analysis) lets time run: the capacitors and inductors of Chapter 5 produce the first- and second-order transients of Chapters 6 and 7, and sinusoidal sources lead to phasors, AC power and three-phase systems, all analysed with the nodal and mesh methods of Chapter 3 extended to complex impedances. Part III (Advanced Network Theory) carries the same matrices into the Laplace domain, describes networks by their terminal parameters as the theorems of Chapter 4 suggest, and replaces the lumped model by the transmission line where Chapter 1 showed it must fail. From Part IV onward, circuits become one realisation of linear time-invariant systems: the linearity that underpins superposition and Thévenin's theorem is the same linearity that makes convolution, Fourier analysis, digital filtering, modulation and feedback control possible. The mathematics of linear algebra, differential equations and transforms is drawn from *The Mathematics Universe*, the field theory behind the lumped model from *Engineering Physics*, and the device physics behind dependent-source models from *Electronics & Computing*.
