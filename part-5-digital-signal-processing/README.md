# Part V — Digital Signal Processing

Part IV ended with sampled signals described exactly: sequences, convolution sums, difference equations and the Z-transform. Part V turns that mathematics into working processing. A hearing aid that removes feedback whistle, a protection relay that measures fault impedance within one mains cycle, a radar that pulls a weak echo out of clutter, an ECG monitor that suppresses hum and baseline wander, a software-defined radio that demodulates a channel from a stream of complex samples — all of them read numbers from a converter, compute, and write numbers out, on time, with finite word lengths. This part asks how that is done well. It treats the processor as a signal-processing system in its own right: how signals and systems are represented in a machine and how finite precision changes them; how a finite block of samples is turned into a spectrum; how filters are specified, designed and implemented; and how sample rates are changed, filters made to adapt, and signals analysed in time and frequency together.

The emphasis throughout is on design that can be verified with numbers. Every method is taken from a specification (passband ripple, stopband attenuation, frequency resolution, SNR, latency, operations per second) to an implementation whose performance can be predicted before it is built and measured after.

By the end of this part you should understand:

- how sampling and quantization, fixed-point and floating-point arithmetic, and the real-time processing chain set the accuracy and cost of any DSP system, and how numerical error is predicted and budgeted;
- what the discrete Fourier transform computes, why the FFT makes it cheap, and how leakage, windowing and zero padding affect what a measured spectrum really shows;
- how FIR and IIR digital filters are specified, designed and implemented, and how their stability and finite-precision behaviour are ensured;
- how multirate processing changes sample rates efficiently, how adaptive filters learn their coefficients, and how spectral, time–frequency and wavelet methods analyse signals whose content changes with time;
- how these tools combine in practical audio, image, biomedical, power-system, radar and communication processing.

Part V sits between the signal theory that precedes it and the engineering destinations that follow. It depends directly on Chapter 19 (sampling, aliasing and the quantization interface) and Chapter 20 (convolution, the Z-transform, poles, zeros and stability), and it reuses the filter vocabulary of Chapter 13 and the frequency-domain ideas of Chapter 18. In return, it supplies the working tools of the rest of the book. Communication systems in Part VI are now largely digital: pulse-code modulation in Chapter 27 is quantization applied to a link, matched filters and equalisers are digital filters, OFDM in Chapter 30 is built on the FFT, and the noise analysis of Chapter 29 relies on the spectral-estimation ideas introduced here. Control systems in Part VII are implemented as difference equations running on processors, and the digital control fundamentals of Chapter 35 inherit the sampling delays, word-length limits and stability checks of this part. The mathematics of the Fourier and Z-transforms and of random processes is recapped where it is used and developed fully in *The Mathematics Universe*; converters, processors and their circuits belong to *Electronics & Computing*.
