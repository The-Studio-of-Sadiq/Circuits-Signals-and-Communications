# Part IV — Signals and Systems

Parts I–III treated circuits as the object of study: given a network, find its voltages and currents. Part IV turns the question around. The voltage on a busbar, the output of an ECG electrode, the echo received by a radar and the samples stored by an audio converter are *signals*, functions that carry information. The amplifier, filter, cable, sensor, digital algorithm or control loop that acts on them is a *system*. This part develops the general theory of both, independent of the hardware that realises them, so that the same ideas serve a power-quality analyser, a hearing aid, a seismometer and a mobile-phone modem. Its central result is that a linear time-invariant system is completely described by one function, its impulse response or equivalently its frequency response, and that continuous signals can be replaced by discrete samples without loss under conditions that can be stated exactly.

By the end of this part you should understand:

- how signals are classified (continuous or discrete, periodic or aperiodic, even or odd, energy or power) and built from steps, impulses, ramps and exponentials;
- what linearity, time invariance, causality, stability and memory mean, and how to test a system for each;
- why the impulse response and convolution determine the output of any linear time-invariant system, and how differential and difference equations describe the same systems;
- how Fourier series and transforms turn convolution into multiplication, and what magnitude, phase, bandwidth, group delay and distortion mean for a signal passing through a system;
- when a continuous signal can be recovered from its samples, what aliasing is, and how anti-aliasing and reconstruction filters work in practice;
- how the Z-transform, poles and zeros describe discrete-time systems and their stability, and how a digital filter is defined.

**How this part connects to the rest of the book.** Part IV generalises what Parts II and III did for circuits. The first- and second-order responses of Chapters 6–7, the phasors of Chapter 8, the Laplace-domain transfer functions of Chapter 11 and the frequency responses of Chapter 13 reappear here as special cases of LTI-system theory, now applied to any system and to discrete as well as continuous time; transmission lines (Chapter 14) become an example of a system with delay and dispersion. Part IV in turn supplies the foundations for everything that follows. Part V (Digital Signal Processing) builds the DFT, digital filters and multirate and adaptive methods directly on Chapters 19 and 20. Part VI (Communication Theory) uses spectra, bandwidth, filtering and sampling to describe modulation, channels and noise, and adds the random-signal models only touched on here. Part VII (Control-System Fundamentals) reuses transfer functions, stability and frequency response to design feedback. The mathematics of the transforms themselves (Fourier, Laplace and Z, and generalised functions such as the impulse) is recapped where needed and developed in full in *The Mathematics Universe*.
